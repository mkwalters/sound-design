01

Warm‑up

Sound in ninety seconds

Three ideas you need before any of the pedals make sense.

Sound is air pressure wiggling back and forth. Your guitar's pickup turns the vibrating string into an electrical signal that wiggles the same way. If you draw that wiggle over time, you get a waveform. Three things about it matter for everything that follows:

  • Height is loudness. A bigger wiggle is a louder sound. This is the amplitude.
  • Speed is pitch. More wiggles per second means a higher note. This is the frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). Your low E string vibrates about 82 times a second.
  • Shape is tone. A smooth wave sounds pure. A jagged one sounds buzzy because it secretly contains extra, higher frequencies called harmonics.

Press play and turn the knobs to hear each idea:

The world of effects looks huge, but every pedal changes one of these five things about the signal. Learn the five and you can make sense of any pedal you run into.

02

Shape

Overdrive, distortion & fuzz

The sound of rock and roll is a waveform with its top cut off.

Turn an amplifier up past what it can handle and it runs out of room. The peaks of the wave hit a ceiling and get flattened. Engineers call this clipping, and in the 1950s it counted as a fault. Then guitarists heard it and loved it. Overdrive, distortion and fuzz pedals all clip on purpose: they boost the signal hard and squash it against a ceiling.

The transfer curve below sums up a drive pedal in one picture. Input level runs along the bottom and output level runs up the side. A straight diagonal means the sound passes through untouched. The more the ends bend over, the more the loud parts get squashed.

Three flavors of the same idea

Overdrive
Gentle, rounded clipping, like a tube amp pushed hard. It responds to how you play: pick softly and it almost cleans up, dig in and it growls. Blues players love it.
Distortion
Harder clipping with much more gain. It's tighter and sustains longer. Think 80s metal and grunge.
Fuzz
Clips so hard that the wave turns almost square, often lopsided. It's buzzy, sputtery and wild, the sound of the 60s (“Satisfaction”, Hendrix).

Look at the harmonics graph. Squaring off a wave adds new frequencies at 2×, 3×, 4×… the note you played. That added energy is the “grit”. Symmetrical clipping mostly adds the odd harmonics (3×, 5×), and lopsided fuzz adds the even ones too.

Try this

  • Start with Drive all the way down on Strummed chords, then sweep it up slowly. Watch the curve bend.
  • Switch to Palm‑muted riff and Fuzz. Instant garage band.
  • Play the chords with heavy distortion. They turn to mush, because every note's harmonics clash. That's why rock players love two‑note power chords.
03

Dynamics

The compressor

An automatic volume knob with very fast hands.

A compressor listens to how loud you're playing. When the signal rises above a threshold, it turns the volume down, by an amount set by the ratio. Afterwards the whole signal gets turned back up. The result: loud notes get tamed, quiet notes come up, and everything sounds more even, punchy and sustained.

  • Threshold: how loud the signal has to get before the compressor starts working.
  • Ratio: how hard it pushes back. At 4:1, a note that goes 8 dB over the threshold comes out only 2 dB over. At 20:1 it's basically a brick wall, which is called a limiter.
  • Attack: how fast it clamps down. A slow attack lets the pick's initial “snap” slip through before the squeeze, which makes the sound punchier.
  • Release: how fast it lets go afterwards.

Watch the dot ride along the curve as you play, and watch the meter on the pedal show how many decibels it's taking away.

Try this

  • Pick the Drum loop, which has quiet “ghost” snare hits. Set threshold around −40 dB and ratio 10:1. The ghost notes jump out, and in the Level over time graph the spiky grey line becomes a flat colored one.
  • On Single note, stomp the compressor on and off. With it on, the note seems to ring longer, because the compressor keeps turning up the fading tail.
  • Crank the attack to 100 ms+ and notice the pick attack poke through. Country “chicken pickin'” and funk players live here.
04

Frequency

Filters, EQ & the wah

Why a wah pedal sounds like it's talking.

Remember harmonics? Every guitar note is really a stack of frequencies, from boomy lows to fizzy highs. A filter lets some of those through and turns others down. The tone knob on your guitar is a filter: a low‑pass that rolls off the highs. An EQ pedal is several filters working together.

A wah is a single filter with a sharp, resonant peak that you sweep up and down with your foot. Your mouth does the same thing when you say “oooo‑aaaah”: your throat and lips move a resonance from low to high. That's why a wah sounds like a voice.

In the graph, the line is the filter's frequency response: lows on the left, highs on the right, spaced like a piano keyboard. Above the middle line gets boosted and below gets cut. The glow behind it is the live sound.

Try this

  • Drag the treadle up and down in time with the chords. You're doing a funk wah.
  • Set Auto to Envelope and play the Lead lick. Now the filter follows how hard each note is picked: an auto‑wah or envelope filter, a staple of 70s funk.
  • Switch to Low‑pass and pull the treadle all the way back. That's what your guitar's tone knob does. Turn up the resonance for a synth‑y squelch.
05

Modulation

Tremolo & vibrato: meet the LFO

A robot hand that turns a knob back and forth, forever.

The next four pedals all rely on movement, and that movement comes from an LFO (low‑frequency oscillator). It's a wave that's too slow to hear, anywhere from once every ten seconds to about 15 times a second. Instead of making sound itself, it turns a knob for you, back and forth.

Point the LFO at the volume and you get tremolo, the throbbing pulse of surf rock and swampy blues. Point it at the pitch and you get vibrato, a singer‑like waver. The graph shows the difference: tremolo makes the wave swell and shrink, vibrato squeezes and stretches it.

Fun fact: Leo Fender mixed these two words up for decades. The “tremolo arm” on a Strat bends pitch (vibrato!), and his amps' “vibrato” channel actually did tremolo. Guitarists still argue about it.

Try this

  • Set Rate to match the beat of the chords. Tremolo works best locked to the tempo.
  • Switch the shape to square and push the depth to 100%: a choppy, stuttering helicopter effect.
  • Try Vibrato with a slow rate and moderate depth. Remember this sound, because the next pedal is built from it.
06

Modulation

Chorus & flanger

One recipe, two very different flavors.

Here's the recipe: make a copy of your signal and delay it by a few milliseconds. Then let an LFO slowly nudge that delay time longer and shorter. A delay that keeps changing makes the pitch wobble, so the copy has vibrato. Finally, mix the wobbly copy back in with the original.

Chorus
A longer delay (15–30 ms) with no feedback. The copy sounds like a second guitarist playing along, slightly out of tune. It's shimmery, lush and wide. Think 80s clean tones and “Come As You Are”.
Flanger
A very short delay (1–5 ms) plus feedback, which sends some of the output back into the delay. At that short gap the two copies interfere: some frequencies cancel out and others add up, in an evenly spaced pattern called a comb filter. As the LFO sweeps, the comb slides up and down and you hear a jet plane.

The name comes from tape machines. Engineers played two identical tapes at once and pressed a thumb on the flange (the rim) of one reel to slow it down slightly.

Try this

  • With headphones on, play Strummed chords through the Chorus preset. The left and right ears wobble in opposite directions, so it sounds huge.
  • Pick Flanger and watch the comb's “teeth” in the graph slide as you hear the whoosh.
  • Turn Feedback negative: the notches and peaks swap places and the tone goes hollow.
07

Modulation

The phaser

A swoosh with no delay at all.

A phaser gets a similar swirl using a different trick. Instead of a delay, it runs the signal through a chain of all‑pass filters. These filters don't make any frequency louder or quieter. They only shift its timing, and they shift some frequencies more than others.

Mix that shifted copy with the original. Wherever a frequency comes out exactly half a cycle out of step, its peaks meet the other copy's troughs and the two cancel. That makes a few deep notches. An LFO sweeps the filters, so the notches glide up and down.

Phaser vs. flanger: a flanger makes many evenly spaced notches, which sounds metallic, like a jet. A phaser makes just a few notches spread unevenly, which sounds softer and more vocal. Every two filter stages add one notch. The classic 4‑stage phaser is all over 70s funk and early Van Halen.

Try this

  • Count the notches in the graph as you switch between 2, 4, 6 and 8 stages.
  • Slow the Rate right down and turn up Resonance for a deep, liquid sweep.
  • Jump back up to the flanger and compare. Once you hear the difference you won't un‑hear it.
08

Time

Delay & echo

Hello… hello… hello…

A delay records what you play and plays it back a moment later. The feedback knob sends that echo back into the delay again, so you hear an echo of the echo of the echo, each one a little quieter.

The graph shows the pedal's impulse response: if you played one tiny click, this is what would come out. The first bar is you, and every bar after it is a repeat.

  • Under 50 ms: doubling. Too fast to hear as an echo, so it just sounds thicker, like two guitars.
  • 80–150 ms: slapback. One quick bounce, the sound of 50s rockabilly and early Elvis.
  • 300–600 ms: echo. Distinct, rhythmic repeats. Set it to a dotted eighth note and your playing turns into a cascade, the way U2's The Edge plays.
  • Tone: old tape echoes and analog delays lost some treble on every repeat. Turning this down gives you that warm, fading, vintage repeat.

Try this

  • Use Single note and step through the presets from Doubling to Ambient.
  • With the Dotted 8th preset, switch to the Lead lick. The echoes fill the gaps between notes.
  • Twist Time while repeats are ringing. The pitch bends just like a tape machine slowing down.
  • Tap the TAP switch in rhythm to set the delay time by hand.
09

Time

Reverb

A room in a box.

Clap your hands in a cathedral. The sound bounces off the walls, floor and ceiling, and thousands of reflections reach your ears later and quieter until they blur into a smooth, fading wash. Reverb is a delay with millions of repeats, too close together to hear one by one.

A reverb has three parts, and you'll see all of them in the graph: the direct sound, a handful of early reflections (they tell your brain how big the room is and what shape), and the tail, the dense wash that fades away. Decay sets how long the tail lasts. Engineers measure it as the time the sound takes to fade by 60 dB.

Room & Hall
Imitate real spaces: tight and natural, or big and lush.
Plate
In 1950s studios this was a giant steel sheet with a speaker on one side and a pickup on the other. Bright, dense and smooth.
Spring
Built into guitar amps: a speaker shakes a metal spring. It's drippy and boingy, the sound of surf guitar. (Kick an amp that has one and it crashes like thunder.)

Pre‑delay adds a short gap before the reverb starts, so your notes stay clear while the room blooms behind them. Damping copies soft surfaces like curtains and people, which soak up the high frequencies first.

Try this

  • Use Single note and step through Room → Hall → Plate. Listen to the size of the space change.
  • Pick Spring with a short decay, then play the Lead lick. Instant surf.
  • Turn Decay all the way up and Mix past halfway. The guitar dissolves into an ambient cloud.

Putting it together

Build a pedalboard

Order matters. A lot.

Here's every pedal you've met, chained together. Stomp them on, tweak them, and use the arrows to rearrange the order. The usual order, from guitar to amp, is:

wah → compressor → drive → modulation → delay → reverb

Why? Drive goes before delay so that each echo is a clean copy of a distorted note. The other way round, the echoes pile up inside the distortion and turn to mud. Reverb goes last because it's the room you're playing in. Rules are made to be broken, though. Shoegaze bands put huge reverbs into fuzz on purpose. Try the Backwards preset to hear what that does.

Take‑away

The cheat sheet

Everything on one page.

Overdrive / Distortion / Fuzz

Clips the waveform, adding harmonics.

Knobs: Drive, Tone, Level

Listen for: grit, growl, sustain, buzz

Compressor

Turns loud parts down so everything is more even.

Knobs: Threshold, Ratio, Attack, Release

Listen for: squish, sustain, steady level

EQ / Wah

Boosts or cuts frequencies. A wah sweeps a resonant peak.

Knobs: Frequency, Resonance (Q)

Listen for: “wah”, vowels, brighter or darker tone

Tremolo / Vibrato

An LFO wobbles the volume (tremolo) or the pitch (vibrato).

Knobs: Rate, Depth, Shape

Listen for: pulsing, throbbing, wavering

Chorus / Flanger

A short, wobbling delayed copy mixed with the original. Flanger is shorter, with feedback.

Knobs: Rate, Depth, Delay, Feedback, Mix

Listen for: shimmer, width (chorus) · jet whoosh (flanger)

Phaser

All‑pass filters plus the dry signal make a few sweeping notches.

Knobs: Rate, Depth, Stages, Resonance

Listen for: soft, liquid, vocal swirl

Delay / Echo

Plays your sound back later, again and again.

Knobs: Time, Feedback, Mix, Tone

Listen for: distinct repeats, slapback, doubling

Reverb

Thousands of blurred reflections: the sound of a space.

Knobs: Decay, Pre‑delay, Damping, Mix

Listen for: room, hall, ambience, wash
